## Georgios Zorbas: the real man behind the legend

Before he was "Alexis Zorba," a name known worldwide, he was **Georgios Zorbas** — born in 1865 in Katafygio, then part of the Ottoman Empire. His early working life took him through fieldwork and woodcutting before he became a miner: from 1889 to 1911 he worked for a French mining company in Stratoniki, in Chalkidiki.[^wiki-george-zorbas]

In 1915, on Mount Athos — where Zorbas had gone intending to become a monk — he met the young writer **Nikos Kazantzakis**. The two became close friends and travelled together to Mani, in the southern Peloponnese, where they worked side by side as miners at Prastova. It was this shared experience that Kazantzakis later reworked into his 1946 novel *Βίος και Πολιτεία του Αλέξη Ζορμπά* ("The Life and Times of Alexis Zorbas"), known in English as *Zorba the Greek* — fictionalizing Georgios into the character "Alexis." Zorbas himself died on 16 September 1941 in Skopje, decades before the character based on him became internationally famous.[^wiki-george-zorbas][^wiki-zorba-novel]

The novel's opening chapters, describing the narrator's first meeting with Zorbas at the port of Piraeus and Zorbas's own account of discovering the santur at the age of twenty, are part of the official Greek state literature curriculum for Gymnasio Grade 9, in the unit on 20th-century literature (1922-1945).[^ebooks-edu-excerpt]

## The 1964 film and the sirtaki

The novel reached its widest audience through **Michael Cacoyannis's 1964 film adaptation**, starring **Anthony Quinn** as Zorba. The film received seven Academy Award nominations, including Best Picture, Best Director, and Best Actor, and won three: Best Supporting Actress (Lila Kedrova), Best Art Direction (black-and-white), and Best Cinematography (black-and-white).[^wiki-zorba-film]

Its most lasting legacy, though, is a dance that did not exist before the film. Anthony Quinn was unable to perform the traditional Cretan pentozali as originally planned, so choreographers devised a simpler step sequence for him instead, set to **Mikis Theodorakis's** score — the dance now known worldwide as the **sirtaki**. That invented dance went on to become one of the most internationally recognized symbols of Greek culture, closely tied to the popular image of "Zorba's dance" as a celebration of living fully in spite of hardship or failure.[^wiki-zorba-film]
