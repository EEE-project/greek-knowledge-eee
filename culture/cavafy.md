## Who was Cavafy

Constantine P. Cavafy was born in **Alexandria in 1863** and died there in **1933**. He left about **154 poems**, in which he often turns to history and mythology to speak about modern man. «Ithaka» -- first published in the journal *Γράμματα* (Grammata), October-November 1911, when Cavafy was 48 -- is perhaps his most famous poem worldwide.[^ebooks-edu-gr-ithaka]

«Ithaka» speaks to Homer's Odyssey: Odysseus's journey home is a [νόστος](../words/νόστος.md) in the epic's own terms, though the word itself doesn't appear in Cavafy's poem — Cavafy transforms that homecoming into an image of every person's journey through life.

## Who they were

**The Laestrygonians** — man-eating giants who destroyed the ships of strangers and killed their crews; a symbol of great dangers and destruction.

**The Cyclopes** — giants with a single eye on the forehead, possessing enormous strength and living in isolation; a symbol of violence, brute force, and the absence of civilization.

**Poseidon** — the god of the sea, Odysseus's enemy (since Odysseus blinded his son Polyphemus); a symbol of the forces we cannot control — nature, fate, circumstance.

For Cavafy, these figures are not just mythological characters — they carry a deeper, symbolic meaning.

## Metaphorical meaning

These figures can symbolize: our fears, anxiety, insecurity, difficulties, problems, people who disappoint us, obstacles we create for ourselves. The poem's own vocabulary of travel and arrival — βγεις, φτάσεις, ταξίδι, δρόμος — covers the same thematic ground as the Odyssey itself, journey and homecoming, even though Cavafy writes in Modern Greek, not Homer's own language.

Konstantinos Petrou Kavafis, known, especially in English, as Constantine P. Cavafy and often published as C. P. Cavafy, was a Greek poet, journalist, and civil servant from Alexandria. A major figure of modern Greek literature, he is sometimes considered the most distinguished Greek poet of the 20th century. His works and consciously individual style earned him a place among the most important contributors not only to Greek poetry, but to Western poetry as a whole.[^wikipedia] This knowledge base itself mixes the two spellings: **Cavafy** here (matching Wikipedia and English-language publishers), **Kavafis** in `texts/kavafis_ithaki/`'s own directory name (matching the created_with_eee course it draws its Greek text from) -- both transliterate the same **Καβάφης**, and Russian-language sources, including the readings below, follow the same closer-to-Greek pattern: **Кавафис**, not a Cavafy-style spelling.

## A mixed poetic language

That "consciously individual style" shows up concretely in «Ithaka»'s own wording, word by word, across all 36 lines.[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

**στ. 1-3.** The poem opens with **Σα**, the colloquial elided form of the conjunction **σαν** "when, as soon as" (not its other, comparative sense "like, as," which always keeps the fuller spelling) -- the same final-**ν** rule already noted for **την**/**δεν**/**μην** in [definite-articles-nom-acc](../grammar/definite-articles-nom-acc.md) drops it here, since the next word, **βγεις**, starts with a consonant outside the keep-set **κ, π, τ, ξ, ψ, γκ, μπ, ντ**.[^cavafy-ithaka-text] Right after it comes **πηγαιμό** (from **πηγαίνω** "to go"), a noun with a bookish, poetic coloring -- ordinary Modern Greek would say **ταξίδι**, **δρόμος**, or **πορεία** for "journey." Set right next to it, the rest of the line is entirely everyday grammar: **εύχεσαι** is the plain 2nd-singular present of **εύχομαι** "to wish," and **να 'ναι** is the ordinary colloquial contraction of **να είναι** "[that it] be." One poetically marked noun inside an otherwise completely demotic sentence.[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

**στ. 4-12.** **Λαιστρυγόνας** and **Κύκλωπας** (accusative plural, each repeated once) keep an older **-ας** ending where everyday Modern Greek now has **-ες** (**Λαιστρυγόνες**, **Κύκλωπες**) -- a genuinely archaizing form, not a spelling variant. Right beside them, **Ποσειδώνα** is named with entirely ordinary modern grammar (the modern accusative of **Ποσειδώνας**, not the classical **Ποσειδῶν**) and modern adjectives, **θυμωμένο** "angry" and **άγριο** "wild, fierce" -- so within the same two lines, the monsters' names lean archaic while the god who threatens them is described in fully contemporary language. **σκέψις** and **συγκίνησις** are older abstract-noun formations in **-ις**, where the modern language now has **-η** (**σκέψη** "thought," **συγκίνηση** "emotion"). And **κουβανείς** -- the demotic/dialectal stem variant of standard **κουβαλάς** "you carry" (see [κουβαλάω](../words/κουβαλάω.md), and the official textbook's own gloss of this exact word) -- sits right after that same run of archaizing/mythological vocabulary, pulling the register back down to the colloquial.[^cavafy-ithaka-text][^ebooks-edu-gr-ithaka]

**στ. 13-23.** The same alternation continues. **πρωιά** is an older, more poetic plural of **πρωί** "morning" (modern usage prefers **πρωινά**); **ευχαρίστησι** is an older-flavored form of **ευχαρίστηση** "pleasure, delight" (the same **-η**/**-ι** doublet seen in pairs like **πόλη**/**πόλι**). **λιμένας** (accusative plural of ancient **λιμήν** "harbour") is archaizing again, against modern **λιμάνια**, and sits beside the compact participial compound **πρωτοϊδωμένους**, "first-seen." **εμπορεία Φοινικικά** -- "Phoenician trading-posts," with the adjective placed after its noun -- gives the phrase a historical, bookish coloring that a neutral phrasing wouldn't have. **τες** (in **τες καλές πραγμάτειες**) is an old/vernacular form of the feminine plural article, where the modern standard has **τις**; **πραγμάτειες** itself just means "goods, wares." The catalog that follows -- **σεντέφια** (mother-of-pearl), **κοράλλια** (coral), **κεχριμπάρια** (amber), **έβενους** (ebony) -- names genuinely non-obvious vocabulary: the official textbook's own footnotes gloss **σεντέφι** and **έβενος** directly, confirming these aren't just archaisms but real gaps in an ordinary reader's vocabulary.[^cavafy-ithaka-text][^ebooks-edu-gr-ithaka] Word order leans archaizing once more in **σε πόλεις Αιγυπτιακές πολλές**, "to many Egyptian cities" with both modifiers trailing the noun instead of preceding it as neutral Modern Greek would put them. **σπουδασμένους**, "the learned, the educated," is a substantivized participle from **σπουδάζω** "to study."[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

**στ. 24-33.** The archaizing/demotic alternation continues to the poem's very end. **φθάσιμον**, "arrival," keeps the neuter singular in **-ον** rather than the everyday demotic **-ο** (**φθάσιμο**) -- a single katharevousa-flavored letter on an otherwise ordinary noun, echoing **σκέψις**/**συγκίνησις** earlier in the poem.[^cavafy-ithaka-text] Right beside it, **ν' αράξεις**, "to anchor, to settle down" (subjunctive of **αράζω**), is plain nautical/colloquial vocabulary, pulling the register back down exactly as **κουβανείς** did earlier.[^cavafy-ithaka-text] The elisions continue too -- **θα 'βγαινες** (**θα έβγαινες**), **σ' έδωσε** (**σε έδωσε**), **τ' ωραίο** (**το ωραίο**) -- the same colloquial contraction already seen in **να 'ναι**.[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

**στ. 34-36.** The closing lines return to the same contrast in a single word: **πτωχική**, "poor," is the learned/katharevousa form, kept in Modern Greek mainly in set phrases (**πτώχευση** "bankruptcy," **πτωχοκομείο** "poorhouse"); the ordinary demotic word for "poor" is **φτωχή**, its regular sound-changed descendant -- the same **πτ-** > **φτ-** shift as **πτερό** > **φτερό** "wing."[^cavafy-ithaka-text] The poem ends on entirely demotic idiom: **δεν σε γέλασε** doesn't mean Ithaka "didn't laugh at you" but "didn't deceive you" -- **γελάω** "to deceive, to trick" is a common Modern Greek idiomatic sense of the same verb that also means "to laugh."[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

Two repetitions work the same alternation at the level of phrasing rather than single words: **ηδονικά μυρωδικά ... ηδονικά μυρωδικά** ("sensuous spices... sensuous spices") and **να μάθεις και να μάθεις** ("learn, and learn again") are not rhetorical symmetry so much as the rhythm of someone circling back to intensify what they've already said.[^cavafy-ithaka-text]

None of this settles into a single, nameable dialect. Cavafy moves between an archaizing, katharevousa-leaning register and plain demotic speech within the same sentence, sometimes the same line -- his own mixed poetic idiolect, not a text written "in" any one variety of Greek.

## Ithaka read aloud

«Ithaka» has also been recorded by performers. Seven readings are on YouTube, linked here and not copied: two readings of the Greek text -- one credited in its title to Grigoris Valtinos (uploaded by the channel 1969anre in 2012),[^ithaka-reading-valtinos] the other an "Official Audio Release" from the channel Ελληνική Ποίηση & Θέατρο ("Greek Poetry & Theatre," 2019)[^ithaka-reading-greek-poetry-theatre] -- two English readings -- Sean Connery's (uploaded by the channel Upgrade Your Mindset in 2021)[^ithaka-reading-connery] and the SpokenVerse channel's, read by Tom O'Bedlam (2011)[^ithaka-reading-obedlam] -- and three Russian readings: Irina Kovalevskaya's audio reading (2023),[^ithaka-reading-kovalevskaya] actor and Vedogon Theatre founder Pavel Kurochkin's reading (2018, uploaded by Eugenia Kritsevskagia),[^ithaka-reading-kurochkin] and an episode of Chersonesos Taurica's «Мой Херсонес» poetry series (2021), which names its translation -- Ильинская's, already cited (reference-only) in `texts/kavafis_ithaki/translations_ru.md`.[^ithaka-reading-chersonesos]
