## A much larger class of verbs governs να than "want to" alone

[na-dependent-verb-forms](na-dependent-verb-forms.md) already introduces **να** as Modern Greek's general substitute for a dependent infinitive; at B1, the class of verbs and expressions that govern it turns out to be large and semantically varied -- obligation (**πρέπει, χρειάζεται, είναι ανάγκη**), possibility/permission (**μπορεί, μπορώ**), intention/plans (**σκοπεύω, πρόκειται, λέω**), hope (**ελπίζω**), proposing (**προτείνω**), and more: **Ελπίζω να έρθετε μαζί μας.** "I hope you'll come with us." **Είναι ώρα να φύγω.** "It's time for me to go."[^ellinika-b-ch11] The subjunctive verb form itself is built with the exact same stem-change rules as the simple future (see [future-tha-aspect](future-tha-aspect.md)) -- only the particle differs, **να** instead of **θα**.[^ellinika-b-ch11]

**ας** ("let, may") triggers the same subjunctive form as an alternative to **να** in hortative/permissive contexts -- offers, suggestions, and reluctant permission: **– Να έρθει κι η Πόπη μαζί μας; – Ας έρθει, αν θέλει.** "– Should Popi come too? – Let her, if she wants."[^ellinika-b-ch11] Negation works the same way as with any **να**-clause: **δε(ν)** before the governing verb negates the whole idea, **μη(ν)** inside the clause negates just the dependent action -- **Σκέφτομαι να μην πάω πουθενά.** "I'm thinking of not going anywhere."[^ellinika-b-ch11]

Because **να** marks mood rather than any one specific meaning, the identical surface form can carry genuinely different senses depending on context alone: **Ο Πάμπλο μπορεί να ταξιδέψει αύριο.** can mean "he *might* travel tomorrow" (possibility), "he's *able to* travel" (ability), or "he *has permission to* travel" (leave) -- nothing in the sentence itself disambiguates which.[^ellinika-b-ch11]
