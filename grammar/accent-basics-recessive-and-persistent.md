## Accent basics: the three marks and where they can stand

Greek marks three accents: **acute** (΄, e.g. **λόγος**), **grave** (`, an acute replaced by a grave when a word with a final acute is followed directly by another word, e.g. **καλὸς ἀνήρ**), and **circumflex** (῀, e.g. **δῶρον**). An accent can only fall on one of the last three syllables of a word — counting from the end, the **ultima**, the **penult**, and the **antepenult** — and where exactly it can land within that window depends on the length (short/long) of the ultima: an acute on the antepenult requires a short ultima, and a circumflex can only stand on a long penult when the ultima is short.[^smyth-accent]

## Two accent behaviors: persistent (nouns) vs. recessive (verbs)

Nouns and adjectives have **persistent** accent: the accent stays on the same syllable it occupies in the nominative singular (the citation form) as the word declines, moving only when a general rule above forces it to (most often because a long-ultima case ending pushes an antepenult acute forward onto the penult). **λόγος**, for instance, keeps its accent on the same syllable throughout most of its paradigm.

Verbs, by contrast, have **recessive** accent: the accent is drawn as far back toward the beginning of the word as the quantity of the ultima allows, regardless of where it fell in some other form of the same verb. **λύω** accents the first syllable because nothing prevents it; a verb form with a long ultima accents no further back than the penult even if a related form of the same verb accents the antepenult.[^smyth-accent]
