## Three-way choice: definite, indefinite, or bare noun

Beyond the definite/indefinite contrast covered at A2 (see [definite-articles-nom-acc](definite-articles-nom-acc.md), [indefinite-article-enas](indefinite-article-enas.md)), Modern Greek regularly drops the article altogether in several recurring contexts: naming someone's profession (**Είναι ηλεκτρολόγος.** "He's an electrician"), a broad class of set/idiomatic verb+noun phrases (**Έχω δίκιο.** "I'm right," **Παίζω πιάνο.** "I play piano," **Κάνω μπάνιο.** "I take a bath"), and institutional/official names that read like a single compound rather than "noun + genitive" (**οδός Δημοκρατίας**, never *οδός της Δημοκρατίας*).[^ellinika-b-ch2]

The same underlying referent can take the definite, indefinite, or no article depending on exactly what's being said about it: **Γνωρίζεις τον Κώστα;** (a specific, already-known Kostas) vs. **Γνώρισα έναν Κώστα.** (one, previously-unknown Kostas) vs. **Τον λένε Κώστα.** (naming, no article at all).[^ellinika-b-ch2]

The definite article is even optional in some predicate-noun positions with no real change in meaning: **Είναι φίλος μου.** and **Είναι ο φίλος μου.** are both printed as equally natural.[^ellinika-b-ch2]
